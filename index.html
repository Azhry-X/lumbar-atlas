Bimanual deep abdominal palpation screening for an expansile retroperitoneal aortic pulsation mimicking mechanical low back or flank pain.

How to perform

  1. Position the patient supine with knees flexed to relax the anterior abdominal wall musculature.
  2. Place both hands flat on the epigastrium/umbilical region slightly left of midline above the aortic bifurcation.
  3. Palpate deeply to detect the lateral borders of the aortic pulsation and distinguish an expansile mass from a transmitted pulse.
  4. Account for abdominal girth (< 100 cm vs ≥ 100 cm) when interpreting palpation sensitivity.

Positive / result

Palpable widened, laterally expansile pulsatile abdominal mass -> urgent vascular ultrasound; if symptomatic with tearing back/abdominal pain or hypotension -> same-day emergency department transfer.

Interpretation

Sensitivity rises with larger aneurysm diameter (≥ 5.0 cm) and waist girth < 100 cm.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Moderate

Fink 2000 Arch Intern Med: Sn 68% (60–76), Sp 75% (68–82), LR+ 2.7, LR− 0.43; Sn 82% for AAA ≥ 5.0 cm; Sn 91% if girth < 100 cm vs 53% if ≥ 100 cm.

Screening and classification criteria for axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) in patients with chronic back pain.

Key points

  1. Screen inflammatory back pain features: insidious onset, morning stiffness improving with exercise, no improvement with rest, and awakening in the second half of the night.
  2. Assess imaging arm (sacroiliitis on MRI or radiographs plus SpA feature) and clinical arm (HLA serology positive plus SpA features: enthesitis, uveitis, dactylitis, psoriasis, IBD, family history, good NSAID response, elevated CRP).
  3. Refer suspected axial spondyloarthritis to rheumatology for diagnostic confirmation.

Positive / result

Fulfilment of ASAS imaging or clinical arm criteria in chronic back pain -> rheumatology referral.

Interpretation

Differentiates inflammatory axial spondyloarthritis from non-specific mechanical low back pain.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Moderate

Rudwaleit 2011: Sn 79.5%, Sp 83.3%.

Same-day emergency neurological triage for suspected cauda equina compression, integrating urinary retention, saddle anaesthesia, bilateral sciatica, post-void residual (PVR) bladder scanning, and surgical timing.

Key points

  1. Screen for new-onset urinary retention, difficulty initiating micturition, impaired urethral sensation, or overflow incontinence.
  2. Screen for new faecal incontinence or loss of anal sphincter tone, and test perianal/perineal saddle sensation.
  3. Assess for bilateral radicular leg pain, progressive lower-limb motor deficit (e.g. foot drop), or gait disturbance.
  4. Perform bladder ultrasound scan to measure post-void residual (PVR) volume alongside clinical neurological assessment.
  5. Activate same-day emergency pathway for urgent MRI and surgical decompression when red flags are present.

Positive / result

Urinary retention (Sn 0.90, Sp 0.99) or PVR > 200 mL (MRI-confirmed CES in 43%), saddle sensory deficit, bowel dysfunction, or progressive bilateral motor deficit -> same-day emergency MRI and surgical referral (decompression < 48 h associated with better outcomes).

Interpretation

Bladder status at the time of surgery is the dominant factor for bladder recovery; decompression < 48 h is associated with better outcomes.

Accuracy & evidence Evidence: Low

Bladder scan: PVR > 200 mL → MRI-confirmed CES in 43%; PVR < 200 mL without objective signs → MRI+ rare (Ann R Coll Surg Engl 2024). Urinary retention: Sn 0.90, Sp 0.99 (classic estimate, Deyo 1992 JAMA). Timing: decompression < 48 h associated with better outcomes (Ahn 2000 meta-analysis). Prognosis: bladder status at the time of surgery is the dominant factor for bladder recovery (Qureshi & Sell 2007).

Guideline recommendations against routine lumbar imaging in non-specific low back pain without serious underlying pathology.

Key points

  1. Complete red-flag triage to exclude fracture, malignancy, infection, cauda equina syndrome, or inflammatory spondyloarthritis.
  2. Explain to the patient that routine imaging is not needed when serious pathology is not suspected and that incidental age-related imaging findings are common.
  3. In specialist settings, consider imaging only if the result is likely to change clinical management.

Positive / result

"Do not routinely offer imaging in a non-specialist setting" (1.1.4); consider imaging in specialist settings "only if the result is likely to change management" (1.1.6).

Interpretation

Routine imaging in non-specific low back pain does not improve clinical outcomes.

Key points

How to perform

  1. Record baseline pain location (most distal extent) and intensity prior to movement testing.
  2. Perform repeated end-range lumbar movements in standing and lying (extension, flexion, and lateral shift/side-glide as indicated).

6-test manual pain provocation composite for ruling in or ruling out the sacroiliac joint as the primary pain generator (after excluding centralisation).

How to perform

  1. First screen lumbar repeated movements to exclude discogenic centralisation.
  2. Perform Distraction test (supine, posterior-lateral pressure on bilateral ASIS).
  3. Perform Thigh Thrust test (supine, hip flexed with posterior shearing force along femur).
  4. Perform Compression test (side-lying, downward pressure over uppermost iliac crest).
  5. Perform Sacral Thrust test (prone, anterior-directed force over mid-sacrum).
  6. Perform Gaenslen test bilaterally (supine, maximal hip flexion on one side with contralateral hip extension over plinth edge).

Interpretation

When all 6 provocation tests are negative, symptomatic SIJ dysfunction is ruled out.

9-item prognostic screening tool stratifying low back pain patients into low, medium, and high risk of persistent disability to match targeted care pathways.

Key points

  1. Administer all 9 items (physical/functional items and psychosocial subscale items 5–9).

9 items; total 0–3 = low risk; psychosocial subscale (items 5–9) 4–5 = high risk; rest = medium.

Dossier page 8 · Loading & RTS

Session-by-session tracking and discharge milestone criteria comparing baseline and follow-up PROMs against validated minimal important change (MIC / MCID) thresholds.

Key points

  1. Record session pain (NPRS), directional preference / centralisation status, neurological re-check, interventions delivered, and loading progression across visits.
  2. At discharge, re-administer baseline PROMs (ODI, RMDQ, NPRS, PSFS, TSK-11) and compute absolute and percentage change.
  3. Benchmark individual recovery against Ostelo 2008 MIC consensus (VAS 15; NRS 2; RMDQ 5; ODI 10; or 30% improvement from baseline) and scale-specific MCIDs.

Positive / result

MIC consensus: VAS 15; NRS 2; RMDQ 5; ODI 10; or 30% improvement from baseline (Ostelo 2008). Modified Oswestry MCID ≈ 6 points (Fritz 2001). RMDQ clinical improvement = 30% reduction from baseline + better on global rating (Jordan 2006). NPRS MDC 2 points; MCID 2.2 (1 week) / 1.5 (4 weeks) (Childs 2005). PSFS MCID 2 points (ROC); 1.4 (SEM method) (Maughan 2010). TSK-11 reduction ≥ 4 points = important change; ICC 0.81, SEM 2.54 (Woby 2005).

Interpretation

Combines patient-reported milestones with objective neurological and movement control restoration to support discharge or specialist referral.
